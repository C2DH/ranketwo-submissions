Code as Cultural Heritage
Script video

Before software engineering had a name, it was women's work. In the early days of computing, programming was largely considered clerical — precise, painstaking, and therefore assigned to women. The six women who programmed ENIAC, one of the first electronic computers, developed foundational techniques before the discipline even had a vocabulary. For decades, their names were systematically excluded from the historical record.

Yet their code survived. Studied by historians alongside archives and testimonies, it becomes a source to reveal the human stories behind the technology.

Code is how we make machines perform tasks. Machines have their own language, but we rarely write in it. Programming languages, translated by other programs, shield us from the machine's inner workings. This is abstraction: freed from those details, we focus on our problem, and code becomes easier to write and less error-prone.

In 1969, Margaret Hamilton directed the team at MIT that wrote the software for the Apollo Guidance Computer — the program that navigated Apollo 11 to the Moon. She coined the term 'software engineering' to give the discipline professional standing. The stacks of printed code beside her in this photograph are roughly her own height.

But what was it like writing them?

*Margaret Hamilton interview extracts*

"It was all men. All men and me."

"He said, how can you work here when you have a baby at home."

"The engineers thought it was funny. There's Margaret again, with her software engineering."

"I found out that some men were making twice as much as women, for no reason."

None of this appears directly in the code. To recover it, and bring the gender dimension into view,  historians had to cross the technical artifact with oral testimony.

Sometimes, a small detail in the code can reveal a larger story. In the Apollo Lunar Module software, one routine carries an unexpected name: BURN_BABY_BURN. On its own, this code is just a label.
But when historians interviewed one of its authors, they uncovered the cultural references behind it : a phrase popularized by Los Angeles radio DJ Magnificent Montague in the 1960s, later associated with the Watts uprising of 1965.

Code invites us to interrogate the technical, social and cultural context of its context of its production. This is historical research in practice : interpret source code in the light of testimonies, archives and further historical evidence. 

But before historians can study code, it has to survive. Software from the 1960s rarely made it through corporate restructuring, technological change, or simple neglect — and source code, as distinct from executable programs, was particularly vulnerable.

The Apollo listings survived because paper copies ended up in the MIT Museum, and because dedicated individuals later undertook the labour of digitisation and transcription."
Narrator: "From punched cards to paper listings, from scanned images to text files and GitHub repository, the code gained a new digital life.

This process can be understood as “reborn digital”: software recovered from surviving physical records and reconstructed in digital form.

Along its journey, this code has passed through three of the major institutions now dedicated to preserving software heritage. The GitHub Archive Program stores code in an Arctic vault designed to last centuries. Beyond the code, the Internet Archive makes many historical programs accessible through browser-based emulation. Software Heritage, a French academic nonprofit, aims to preserve all publicly available source code.

Every layer of code that built our digital world carries decisions, assumptions, and lives. Learning to read it historically means learning to ask not just what it does, but who wrote it, under what conditions, and what other sources needed to fully understand it. This lesson will show you how.
